The one idea

Stop being cheaper or costlier. Be the supplier who keeps the site moving.

Do not sell a bag. Sell a pour that finishes on time.

What the buyer compares today

  • Rate per bag
  • Total on the quotation
  • Credit days on offer

What you make them compare

  • Cost of late loads and idle labour
  • Rework from batch variation
  • Time taken to fix a problem
  • Paperwork for quality audits
  • Hours spent chasing the supplier
  • Price is visible on paper. Delays, rework and chasing are hidden. Your job is to make the hidden costs visible.
  • People pay a little more for certainty, but only after you have proved it.
  • You earn a higher price in stages: trial first, then second supplier, then main supplier.
  • Never attack the rival. Ask questions and let the customer say where the pain is.

Break-even test

How many idle days does your higher rate need to pay back?

Change the numbers. The figures below are examples. In a real meeting, ask the site team for their own cost of a stopped day. A number they say themselves convinces better than a number you bring.

The memory hook

C.E.M.E.N.T. The six moves that win an account

Say the word to yourself before every customer call. Each letter is one move. You do not need all six in the first meeting, but you should know which one you are using.

C

Find the hidden cost

Cost of delay

  • Ask what one late truck or one stopped pour costs in labour, machines and schedule.
  • Look for faults in the current supply: late loads, batch variation, slow complaint handling.
  • Let them say the problem. Do not run down the rival.
  • Turn their answer into rupees, then compare it with your price gap.

Say it“What did the last delayed pour cost you?”

E

Reach everyone who feels the cement

Every decision maker

  • Procurement buys on price. Site engineer, project manager and quality head live with the result.
  • Meet all of them in the first month. You need allies who feel the pain of bad supply.
  • On big accounts, reach the owner or director at the right moment.
  • Keep procurement informed. Never go around them rudely.

Say it“Who else is affected if a load does not turn up?”

M

Make the yes small

Mini trial pour

  • Ask for one pour, one slab or one week. Small and low risk is easy to approve.
  • Prepare it like an exam: stock reserved, truck timing confirmed, test certificate ready, you on site.
  • Agree the pass marks before you start, in writing.
  • Afterwards send a one-page summary: on-time loads, batch reports, any issue and the fix.

Say it“Give us one pour. If we miss, you lost nothing.”

E

Be the backup first

Even split of supply

  • Do not ask them to drop the current supplier. Ask to be second source for 20 to 30 percent of volume.
  • A backup lowers their risk, so it is easy to approve.
  • Perform, then ask for 40 percent, then 50, then main supplier at the review.
  • Track your share of their total cement purchase like a score.

Say it“I am not asking you to drop anyone. I want to be your backup.”

N

Trade, never give

No blind discount

  • A price cut teaches them to ask again, lowers your margin for good and is hard to reverse.
  • Trade value for commitment: quarterly volume rebate, better credit days, priority supply in peak season.
  • Every give needs a get. Ask what they can commit to in return.
  • If you must flex on rate, flex on volume tiers, not the base rate.

Say it“I cannot cut the rate. I can pay a rebate on every tonne above your commitment.”

T

Prove it with paper and people

Technical backup and track record

  • Offer mix design help, on-site testing support and batch test certificates.
  • Give one named person who picks up at 6 AM (you), plus a dispatch contact.
  • Keep records: loads delivered on time, complaints closed, time taken to solve.
  • Bring references from sites like theirs. Proof beats promises.

Say it“Here is our delivery record at three similar sites.”

Key concepts

Words you should be able to explain in one breath

Non-trade
Direct sales to large consumers such as developers, infrastructure projects, ready-mix plants and big contractors. Trade means sales through dealers and retailers.
Total cost of ownership
Price per bag plus delays, rework, wastage, chasing and the cost of money tied up in credit. The real cost of buying cement.
Value selling
Selling the outcome (pours that finish on time, fewer problems) before the product itself.
Second source
A backup supplier the buyer keeps to reduce risk. Your easiest way in through a locked door.
Trial order
A small, defined test with pass marks agreed in advance, so the result is a fact and not an opinion.
Volume rebate
Money paid back when the buyer crosses an agreed volume in a period. It protects your base rate.
Credit days
Time the customer takes to pay. Longer credit is a hidden price cut, because your money is stuck with them.
Switching cost
The effort and blame a buyer takes on when changing supplier. Reduce it with trials, support and clear records.
Share of wallet
Your percentage of the customer's total cement purchase. Grow it step by step.
Decision map
Who uses the cement, who influences, who approves, who pays. Draw it for every big account.
Batch test certificate
Paper proof of quality for each batch. Quality teams love it, and it ends many arguments.
Strength and setting time
The two quality numbers site engineers watch most: 28-day strength and how fast the mix sets.

First-meeting question list

Ask before you pitch

Good questions do your selling. The customer hears their own problems out loud, and you collect the numbers you need for the break-even test.

Supply

  • How many loads arrive in a week, and how often is one late?
  • What happens on site when a load is late?
  • Do you keep buffer stock? What does that cost you?

Quality

  • Any problems with strength, setting time or lumps in the last six months?
  • How do you test batches today?
  • What would your quality head like to see more of?

Support

  • When something goes wrong, who do you call?
  • How long does it take to get a real answer?
  • Does the supplier ever visit the site?

Money

  • What payment terms do you have now?
  • Would better terms matter more to you than the rate?
  • What is a stopped day worth to you?

Future

  • What is the next phase of this project, and its monthly need?
  • What would make you add a second supplier?
  • When does your current arrangement come up for review?

Decision map

Procurement may say no while the site engineer is begging for a better supplier. Know what each person cares about and speak to that.

Purchase head

Win them with

Site engineer

Cares about

Loads on time, steady quality, no excuses.

Win them with

Agreed delivery slots, your mobile number, quick fixes on the day.

Project manager

Cares about

Win them with

Quality head

Cares about

Cares about

Practical situations

Fifteen real moments from the field

Tap a situation to see what is going on, what to do, and the line to say. Filter by type to practise one skill at a time.

PriceA rival is ₹10 a bag cheaper and procurement wants a match

What is going on

The buyer uses the rival's rate as the benchmark. If you agree to match it, you have accepted that you are the same as the rival, and you will be asked again next month.

What to do

  • Do not counter with a number. Ask for total volume and how long it will run.
  • Convert the gap into monthly rupees: ₹10 × 20 bags per tonne × 1,000 tonnes is ₹2,00,000 a month.
  • Compare that with what delays cost them. Use the break-even test.
  • Offer a rebate, credit terms or priority supply instead of a lower base rate.

Say itThat gap is about ₹2 lakh a month on your volume. Let's see if one avoided stoppage covers it.

Watch outNever cut the rate in the first meeting. A price you drop is very hard to raise again.

PeopleProcurement says no, but the site engineer likes you

What is going on

The person who lives with the cement wants a better supplier. The person who signs the order only sees the rate.

What to do

  • Ask the engineer to request a trial on one non-critical pour.
  • Give the engineer real facts, such as batch reports and delivery times, to take to procurement.
  • Keep procurement in every email. Respect their role.
  • If the engineer's pain is real, bring in the project manager so the request carries weight.

Say itI'd like to support your team on one pour, so procurement has real data and not my promises.

Watch outGoing over procurement's head in a rude way makes an enemy who can block you for years.

DeliveryYour first trial load arrives late

What is going on

The one thing you promised has failed on the one day everyone is watching. How you react decides whether you get a second chance.

What to do

  • Call before they call you.
  • Own it. Give the real cause and the fix in two sentences.
  • Offer a priority slot or a backup truck on the next order.
  • Send a short written note the same day.
  • Prevent it next time: confirm stock, truck, driver number and slot the evening before every trial load.

Say itWe missed the slot. Here is why, and here is what changes from tomorrow.

Watch outExcuses. A failure handled well can build more trust than a smooth first order.

MoneyThe customer asks for 90 days of credit

What is going on

Credit is a price cut in disguise. It is easy to agree to, and costly in cash flow.

What to do

  • Know your company's credit limit before you walk in.
  • Work out the cost: 60 extra days on ₹10 lakh at 12 percent a year costs about ₹20,000.
  • Trade credit for a bigger volume commitment, an advance, or security such as a bank guarantee or post-dated cheques.
  • Get finance approval before you promise anything.

Say itI can stretch the terms if your volume commitment grows with it.

Watch outLong credit to a slow payer is the fastest way to lose money on a big account.

QualityCube tests come back low, or bags arrive with lumps

What is going on

A quality complaint is your biggest risk and your biggest chance. Customers remember how you behaved far longer than they remember the fault.

What to do

  • Respond the same day and visit the site.
  • Collect the batch number, storage conditions, mix details and curing practice.
  • Bring your technical team and share the batch test certificate.
  • If the fault is yours, replace and say sorry once. If it is on site (storage, water ratio, curing), show it with data, politely.

Say itLet's check batch, storage and mix together. I'll bring our technical team tomorrow morning.

Watch outBlaming the customer without evidence, or hiding a batch problem. Either one ends the relationship.

DeliveryMonsoon or festival season and cement is short

What is going on

Supply is tight and every customer wants priority. A missed pour in peak season undoes months of work.

What to do

  • Ask key accounts for a pour plan two to four weeks ahead.
  • Reserve allocation early for accounts that gave you trials and commitments.
  • Tell customers early if supply will be tight. Bad news early is a gift.
  • Promise only what you can truck.

Say itGive me your next month's pour plan today and I'll lock the trucks.

Watch outOverpromising to win one more order, then failing three accounts at once.

PriceA rival drops the price sharply at quarter end

What is going on

Competitors push volume at month end or quarter end. Customers will hold up the sudden low rate to test you.

What to do

  • Do not panic and discount. Check the customer's real volume plan.
  • Remind them of your service record with a fact or two.
  • If needed, offer a short-period volume incentive with a clear target, and get your manager's approval first.
  • Report the market move to your own boss the same day.

Say itTheir rate is good for this month. Let's talk about what keeps your site covered all year.

Watch outChasing every price dip destroys your margin and teaches customers to wait for the next dip.

PeopleThe customer is tied to a rival by a contract

What is going on

You cannot win the base volume today, but a contract rarely covers everything.

What to do

  • Ask when the contract ends and how renewal is decided.
  • Look for volume outside it: extra requirements, new phases, other sites.
  • Start the relationship three to four months before renewal.
  • Get on the approved vendor list for the next tender.

Say itWhen does the contract come up? I'd like to be in the room when you review it.

Watch outNever push a customer to break a contract. It is unethical and it will be used against you later.

EthicsYou hear a rival pays the purchase manager on the side

What is going on

It may be true, it may be gossip. Either way, you cannot win by matching it, and you must not.

What to do

  • Do not match it and do not accuse anyone.
  • Keep selling on value, service and proof, in writing.
  • Build relationships with the quality head and the owner or director, who care about long-term risk.
  • Tell your manager or compliance team if it affects the deal. Keep notes.

Say itWe keep our dealings clean. Here is what we offer, in writing.

Watch outOne kickback from your side can cost you the job and your company's name.

PeopleYour dealers complain you sell direct in their area

What is going on

Non-trade and trade overlap near big projects. Dealers feel you are taking their business.

What to do

  • Check your company's channel policy before anything else.
  • Be open with dealers about which accounts you handle directly and why.
  • Where policy allows, give the dealer the small local top-up business around the site.
  • Keep one clear rule for who covers which account.

Say itThe big project volume is handled by my team. Your area keeps its regular counter and small-site business.

Watch outLosing dealer goodwill. They are your base business, and they talk to each other.

MoneyAn account you won is paying late

What is going on

Winning an order is half the job. Unpaid invoices turn a good account into a bad one.

What to do

  • Remind them before the due date, not after.
  • Reconcile invoices and fix any dispute fast.
  • Escalate to the accounts head, then the owner, in a polite order.
  • Agree a clear payment plan. Follow your company's rule on supply against overdue amounts.

Say itI want your site fully covered. I need the payment by the 15th to release next week's loads.

Watch outLeaving collections for later. Old dues only get harder to collect.

Price"Send me a rate. I'm comparing five suppliers."

What is going on

A pure price shopper. A bare rate gives you no chance to show value.

What to do

  • Send the rate, but with conditions: volume, delivery slots, payment terms.
  • Ask for 20 minutes with the site team first, so the quote fits the real need.
  • Ask what else decides it besides price.
  • Follow up in two days. If it is only about price, offer a trial and move on without begging.

Say itA rate without your volume and schedule is a guess. Can I see the schedule first?

PeopleThe trial went well, but they still give you only 20 percent

What is going on

Buyers move slowly. A small share is a sensible safety margin for them, not a rejection.

What to do

  • Ask what stops them giving more.
  • Request a review meeting and bring numbers: on-time percentage, quality records, issues closed.
  • Propose a stepped increase, such as 20 to 30 to 40 percent over a quarter.
  • Ask about the next phase or new site. New work often goes to the supplier who performed.

Say itOn the last twelve loads we were on time twelve times. What would it take to move to 40 percent?

PeopleYou cannot even get a first meeting

What is going on

Busy gatekeepers protect procurement's time. A generic "let's meet" email is easy to ignore.

What to do

  • Go through the site. Meet the engineer or foreman at the gate or during a pour.
  • Ask a mutual contact for a short introduction.
  • Bring something useful: a short note on local supply, price trend or monsoon planning.
  • Ask for 15 minutes only, and keep to it.

Say itFifteen minutes. I'll bring one idea to reduce your delivery risk.

PriceCement prices rise and the customer wants a fixed rate

What is going on

The buyer wants protection from market moves. You carry the risk if you agree to hold a low rate for too long.

What to do

  • Offer a validity period, such as 15 days, on a confirmed quantity.
  • Or link price to the market with a cap, agreed in writing.
  • Get finance or your manager to approve any long fixed-rate offer.
  • Be open about why the market is moving, and show your math.

Say itI can hold this rate for 15 days on confirmed volume.

Watch outLocking a rate for months without approval. One price spike can wipe out the profit on the whole account.

Objection handling

When they say this, you say that

“Your rate is higher.”

Yes, by about ₹X a bag. Let's see what one late load costs you. If delays cost more than the gap, the gap is cheap.

“We are happy with our supplier.”

Good, keep them. I only want to be your second source, so one problem never stops your site.

“Match their price and we'll talk.”

I can't match it without cutting service. I can offer a rebate or credit terms that bring your net cost closer.

“Just send a quotation.”

Happy to. Can I spend 20 minutes with your site engineer first, so the quote fits your real needs?

“We have a contract with them.”

When does it end? Meanwhile, can we cover volume outside the contract or a new phase?

“Why should we trust you?”

You shouldn't yet. Try one pour. Here are references from sites like yours.

“A new supplier failed us before.”

What went wrong? Let me show how we handle that, with a named dispatcher and batch reports for every load.

“Give me a discount for this big order.”

I can give a quarterly rebate once the volume is crossed. What volume can you commit to?

Rules of the road

Do this. Never do that.

Do

  • Ask questions for the first ten minutes.
  • Convert every gap into rupees per month.
  • Meet the site team, not only procurement.
  • Prepare each trial like an exam.
  • Call first when something goes wrong.
  • Put every promise in writing.
  • Keep a record of loads, complaints and fixes.
  • Trade value for commitment.

Don't

  • Run down the rival's cement or people.
  • Cut the rate in the first meeting.
  • Promise a delivery you cannot truck.
  • Go around procurement rudely.
  • Give long credit without finance approval.
  • Blame the customer without evidence.
  • Chase every one-month price dip.
  • Offer or accept anything improper.
